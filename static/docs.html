Hevy Fatigue — Methodology & Reference

A technical walkthrough of the fatigue monitoring model: how training load is quantified, how metrics are computed, and the research behind each design decision.

01

Design philosophy

Current daily recommendations: The 0–20 raw score combines 80% subjective fatigue with 20% prior seven-day tonnage relative to the 180-day weekly baseline. Worsening takes effect immediately once monitoring is established. Recovery retains 65% prior fatigue and 35% today's score; recommending more training requires two consecutive supporting check-ins and advances one level at a time.

Pattern Stress update: Load now retains residual acute stress below the chronic baseline and continues to include completed-day training during monitoring gaps. Soreness is your exact 0–4 input. A separate Trend uses the higher of load/soreness, rises immediately and recovers with 65% prior / 35% current signal. Unknown ratings remain unknown; the trend requires sufficient training history and monitoring continuity. These provisional display rules do not measure physiological fatigue.

Continue Training as Normal: Missing or incomplete check-ins, an insufficient workout baseline, and monitoring reacquisition receive this label with an explanation. Startup and returns after more than seven days without valid monitoring require three consecutive complete check-ins. The workout minimum is three positive-tonnage training dates with at least 28 days since the oldest in the prior 180 days. Monitoring breaks preserve training history. These are provisional evidence rules, not measured recovery timelines. Joint advisories remain separate.

Hevy Fatigue is a subjective-first fatigue monitoring dashboard. Daily check-in scores are the primary signal — training load data from Hevy acts as a contextual modifier, not the core driver. The system is designed to surface clear information so that a fatigue-aware athlete can make their own decisions. It does not prescribe training adjustments.

This philosophy was chosen because objective training load metrics (volume, tonnage, sRPE) are well understood to be incomplete proxies for actual systemic stress. Factors like sleep quality, nutrition, life stress, and individual adaptation rate are captured better through subjective self-assessment than through bar-on-back math alone.

Core principle

A fatigue number without subjective context is noise. Subjective scores without load context lack resolution. The system combines both.

02

Check-in model

Each day, the athlete logs recovery signals on a 0-4 scale (0 = fresh / no issue, 4 = most fatigued / most limited). The backend normalizes these values to build a subjective fatigue component.

Field What it captures Role in model
tiredness Overall tiredness and lethargy Primary subjective fatigue input
perceived_recovery How recovered the athlete feels Primary subjective fatigue input
sore_quad_dom Knee-dominant soreness signal Contributes to soreness composite in fatigue formula
sore_posterior Hip/posterior-chain soreness signal Contributes to soreness composite in fatigue formula
sore_upper_push Upper-body push soreness signal Contributes to soreness composite in fatigue formula
sore_upper_pull Upper-body pull soreness signal Contributes to soreness composite in fatigue formula
joint_upper / joint_lower Upper and lower joint discomfort signals Advisory only - excluded from fatigue formula
Design note — joint health

Joint health is excluded from the fatigue formula intentionally. Joint discomfort is a different biological signal from systemic fatigue — it reflects tissue loading tolerance and injury risk, not necessarily overall recovery status. Including it in a weighted average risks suppressing the fatigue number when joint pain is the dominant concern, or inflating it when a minor ache is present alongside genuine freshness. It is surfaced as a standalone advisory on the dashboard instead.

Daily fatigue score

The subjective component is computed from normalized 0-4 check-in values, then scaled to a 0-20 fatigue score where 20 = most fatigued:

// Normalize each check-in field from 0-4 to 0.0-1.0
t = tiredness / 4
r = perceived_recovery / 4
s = (sore_quad_dom + sore_posterior + sore_upper_push + sore_upper_pull) / 16

// Subjective-first weighting from main.py
subjective_fatigue = (0.45 * t) + (0.30 * r) + (0.25 * s)

// Scale to 0-20
daily_fatigue_score = subjective_fatigue * 20

What each input contributes

The subjective total is a weighted blend. The contribution split is fixed and visible below:

Input groupShare of subjective totalMax points out of 20
Tiredness45%9.0
Perceived recovery30%6.0
Soreness composite (all 4 soreness fields together)25%5.0

Inside the soreness composite, each soreness field contributes equally, so each field can contribute up to 1.25 points of the 20-point subjective total.

Scientific basis

The structure of this composite is consistent with the Hooper Index (Hooper & Mackinnon, 1995 [9]) and the DALDA (Daily Analysis of Life Demands for Athletes), both peer-reviewed subjective wellness tools used in applied sports science. Weighted composites of self-report items are the standard approach in athlete monitoring. The specific weights here (45/30/25) are practitioner-selected, not statistically derived from individual data — this is normal practice. Commercial monitoring tools including WHOOP and HRV4Training make the same class of assumption.

This subjective score is then blended with your recent training volume to give the combined score shown on the dashboard (see §08b Fatigue score).

02b

No check-in — pending state

When today has no check-in, the Readiness card shows a banner — No check-in recorded today. Readiness scores may be incomplete. — with a Check in button that opens the check-in form.

  • No score is invented for the missing day. The Subjective / Objective / Combined breakdown is hidden until you check in.
  • In Pattern stress, Soreness and Trend show — for the day. Load still updates from your training history.
  • On the Fatigue Trend chart the missing day is a gap in the line, not a zero.
  • A missed day resets the run of consecutive check-ins. A gap of more than seven days restarts monitoring, which needs three consecutive complete check-ins before recommendations return.
Design rationale

Missing data stays missing. Filling a gap with a neutral or assumed value would make the score look more certain than it is, so the app shows — or Continue Training as Normal with an explanation instead.

03

RPE & session RPE (sRPE)

Two rating systems are used depending on session type:

RPE — Rate of Perceived Exertion (Borg-derived, 0–10)

Used for individual strength and hypertrophy sets. In the powerlifting / RTS context popularized by Mike Tuchscherer, RPE describes proximity to failure on a given set:

RPEMeaning
10Maximum effort — no reps left in reserve (0 RIR)
9Could have done 1 more rep (1 RIR)
8Could have done 2 more reps (2 RIR)
7Could have done 3 more reps (3 RIR)
6Could have done 4+ reps; moderate effort
<6Light / warm-up effort
Reference

RPE for resistance training was formalized by Tuchscherer [1] as an adaptation of Borg's original perceived exertion scale [2]. The reps-in-reserve framing (RIR) is now widely used in sport science [3].

sRPE — Session RPE

Used for conditioning and cardio sessions. sRPE is a single whole-session perceived effort rating (1–10) collected after the session ends, multiplied by duration to yield a session load score. This method was developed by Foster et al. [4] and has extensive validation across endurance and team sports.

session_load = sRPE × duration_minutes

In Hevy Fatigue, sRPE is encoded in session titles using the @N delimiter convention (e.g. CON @7 = conditioning session at sRPE 7).

04

Stress calculation pathways

Four distinct pathways calculate training stress depending on session modality. Each produces a session stress score (SSS) in arbitrary units (AU) that feeds into the EWMA model.

Pathway A — Strength & hypertrophy

Stress is computed per set from intensity and reps, not from the weight on the bar. Intensity is the set's percentage of your estimated 1RM, read from the RPE chart using the set's reps and RPE (or 10 − RIR). Without an RPE, the app estimates it from your best recent e1RM for that exercise (last 12 weeks), or from the Wendler formula as a last resort.

// pct = percentage of e1RM (0.0–1.0) from the RPE chart
central_stress = pct² × reps
peripheral_stress = pct × reps

// Session stress = sum over all working sets
session_stress = Σ (central_stress + peripheral_stress)

Squaring intensity for central stress means heavy, near-maximal sets cost much more than lighter sets of the same reps, while peripheral stress grows in line with volume at intensity. Two sets with the same RPE and reps get the same stress whatever the weight, so an exercise that felt harder should be logged at a higher RPE.

Pathway A2 — HYP sRPE fallback

When a verified HYP session has duration and sRPE logged but at least half of its sets are missing RPE values, the session falls back to a session-level sRPE proxy. This covers circuit-style hypertrophy work and pump sessions where per-set RPE is often incomplete.

// Trigger: ≥50% of sets in the HYP session lack RPE values
fallback_load = (sRPE × duration_minutes) / HYP_SRPE_SCALE

// HYP_SRPE_SCALE = 29.0 in app settings defaults
session_stress = fallback_load
Why this exists

Hypertrophy sessions often blend circuits, short rests, and pump work that do not map cleanly to per-set RPE logging. Session-level sRPE gives the model a reasonable proxy so those workouts still contribute to stress without requiring perfect set-by-set input.

Pathway B — Conditioning

Uses Foster's sRPE method, then normalizes to a target AU range to keep conditioning sessions commensurable with strength sessions on the same scale:

raw_load = sRPE × duration_minutes

// Normalize with conditioning_scale (default 29.0)
session_stress = raw_load / conditioning_scale
Calibration note

The conditioning scaling factor is currently set conservatively and will be refined against real session data. The goal is that a hard 45-minute conditioning session at sRPE 8 produces a session stress score roughly comparable to a demanding strength session — not identical, but within the same order of magnitude so neither modality dominates CTL unfairly.

Pathway C — Cardio

Uses the same sRPE × duration normalization as conditioning, but applies a flat 30% central / 70% peripheral stress split and contributes zero stress to any pattern bucket. Cardio affects global ATL/CTL only.

// Cardio reuses the same conditioning_scale normalization
session_stress = (sRPE × duration_minutes) / conditioning_scale
05

Session modality classification

Session type determines which stress pathway and which movement pattern distribution applies. Detection is two-layer:

Layer 1 — Title keyword matching: Hevy session titles follow a convention using codes that are parsed first. Mixed sessions use the + operator.

CodeModalityStress pathway
STStrength (max effort)Pathway A
HYPHypertrophyPathway A
CONConditioning (CrossFit-style, Strongman)Pathway B
CARCardio (running, cycling, rowing, etc.)Pathway C
ST+CONMixed strength + conditioningPathway A + B

Layer 2 — Exercise-level analysis: If no recognized code is found in the title, the system analyzes the exercises in the session to infer the most likely modality. This handles legacy sessions and sessions logged without title conventions.

sRPE encoding

For conditioning sessions, the sRPE value is encoded in the title after the @ delimiter: CON @7 means a conditioning session at sRPE 7. If no @N is present, a default sRPE is applied and flagged.

05b

Workout title tagging convention

Hevy Fatigue reads session titles from the Hevy app to determine modality and sRPE. For the system to classify your sessions correctly, titles should follow this convention. Sessions without recognized codes are still processed — the classifier falls back to exercise-level analysis — but title tagging is the most reliable path.

Modality codes

CodeUse forExample title
STMax effort strength (powerlifting-style, heavy singles/triples)ST — Back Squat
HYPHypertrophy (moderate load, higher rep, bodybuilding-style)HYP — Upper Push
CONConditioning — CrossFit-style WODs, Strongman, metabolic workCON @8 — Yoke + Farmers
CARCardio — running, cycling, rowing, machine-based aerobic workCAR @6 — Easy Run

sRPE encoding for conditioning and cardio

Conditioning and cardio sessions require a session RPE value to calculate load. Encode it in the title using the @N delimiter, where N is your whole-number sRPE (1–10):

// Format: [CODE] @[sRPE] — [description]
CON @8 — Devil Press AMRAP
CAR @5 — 30min Easy Row

If no @N is present on a conditioning or cardio session, the system applies a default sRPE and flags the session in the verification queue for manual review.

Mixed sessions

Use the + operator to indicate a session that spans two modalities — for example, a strength block followed by a conditioning finisher:

ST+CON @7 — Deadlift + Prowler

Mixed sessions are detected and flagged in the verification queue with a note explaining the split. Each component's stress is calculated through its respective pathway.

Strongman sessions

Strongman training (yoke, farmers, log press, atlas stones, etc.) is classified under CON. The conditioning stress pathway handles the mixed aerobic/strength demand of event training better than the per-set RPE model used for ST/HYP.

No code present?

If a session title has no recognized code, the classifier analyzes the exercises in the session to infer modality. The inferred result and a confidence score are shown in the verification queue — you confirm or correct it before it affects your metrics. See §05c Workout importer for details on the verification queue.

05c

Workout importer

Hevy Fatigue connects to the Hevy API to pull your workout history into the local database. It does not sync continuously in the background.

How sync works

Submitting a daily check-in syncs automatically. To sync by hand, open Menu → Settings → Hevy Sync and press Sync Now. Each sync asks Hevy only for workouts added, changed or deleted since the last sync (the workouts/events endpoint) rather than re-fetching your entire history. The very first sync fetches your full history.

Only one sync runs at a time; while one is running, the sync buttons stay inactive.

Verification queue

Imported sessions that need a decision land in the Session Verification Queue at the top of Menu → Workouts; the Workouts entry shows a badge with the number waiting. A session is verified automatically when it has a duration and the classifier is confident (at least 87% by default) — and, for conditioning or cardio, when its sRPE came from the title. Everything else waits for you.

For each queued session you will see:

  • The detected modality and confidence score
  • A note if a mixed session was detected or sRPE was missing
  • A dropdown to correct the modality if the classifier got it wrong
  • A duration field and conditional sRPE field for conditioning/cardio sessions

Strength and hypertrophy sets count toward stress from their logged RPE as soon as they are imported. Conditioning and cardio sessions, and the hypertrophy sRPE fallback, count only once the session is verified, because they depend on a confirmed duration and sRPE.

Reclassification without re-syncing

If you update the title tagging convention on old sessions in Hevy, or want to re-run the classifier after a settings change, use Reclassify Sessions on the Diagnostics page (Menu → Settings → Open Engine Diagnostics). This re-runs the classifier against all locally stored sessions without contacting Hevy. Verified sessions keep their classification; Force Reclassify All overwrites them too and asks for confirmation first.

Force Full Sync (recovery)

Normally, Sync Now is incremental: it pulls only changes since the last sync, which is fast and API-friendly. In rare cases — after a data integrity issue, a botched canonical rename, or if you suspect orphaned or stale sessions — you can perform a Force Full Sync to delete and reimport your entire workout history from Hevy.

Important: Force Full Sync deletes all imported sessions and sets, then re-downloads everything. Your daily check-ins, canonical exercise names, and the sRPE, type, duration and verified marks you entered for each workout are kept. When you click Force Full Sync, a confirmation dialog appears. Confirm only if you understand the implications.

Force Full Sync is found in Menu → Settings → Hevy Sync, below Sync Now and styled as a danger button to discourage accidental use. Once initiated, both the Sync Now and Force Full Sync buttons become inactive until the reimport completes. If a full sync fails partway through (e.g., API timeout), an error message will tell you to retry or restore from backup — the system never silently leaves you with an empty workout table.

Diagnostics page

The Diagnostics page (/static/diagnostic.html) provides a lower-level view of what the engine is working with: today's score breakdown, check-in inputs, the 7-day volume against your 6-month weekly average, ATL/CTL/TSB, joint advisory state and how your last 10 sessions were classified. If something looks wrong in your metrics and you're not sure why, diagnostics is the first place to look — it shows you exactly what data the system is working with. See §10 Diagnostics page for the full workflow.

Movement pattern buckets

To track fatigue at a finer granularity than "total load," session stress is distributed across four movement pattern buckets. This allows the system to detect, for example, that posterior chain fatigue is accumulating even when overall training volume appears moderate.

Knee dominant
Squat, lunge, leg press patterns
Hip dominant
Deadlift, hinge, hip extension
Push
Press, push-up, dip patterns
Pull
Row, pull-up, curl patterns

Each exercise in exercise_mappings carries percentage weights across the four pattern columns (pct_quad_dom, pct_posterior, pct_upper_push, pct_upper_pull). Session stress is apportioned to each pattern bucket proportionally based on these weights. For conditioning sessions where no exercise mappings exist, the system falls back to an equal 25/25/25/25 distribution across all four buckets.

Each pattern bucket runs its own parallel EWMA, producing per-pattern ATL, CTL, and TSB. This powers the Pattern stress card and the Per-Pattern Fatigue Trend chart on the dashboard.

Pattern stress card

The dashboard's Pattern stress card is a table with one row per pattern (Knee, Hip, Push, Pull) and three columns, each shown as a word plus a fill bar:

ColumnWhat it shows
LoadRecent training on that pattern relative to its own baseline, using training through yesterday. It decays on rest days and keeps showing leftover acute load even below baseline.
SorenessYour latest soreness rating for that pattern, unchanged: None, Mild, Moderate, Significant or Severe (0–4).
TrendThe higher of Load and Soreness, smoothed. It rises immediately and eases off as you recover (65% yesterday's trend, 35% today's signal).
// Load signal per pattern (target-day training removed first)
load = clamp(ATL / (2 × CTL), 0.0, 1.0)
soreness = rating / 4

// Trend: rises at once, recovers gradually
trend = max(raw, 0.35 × raw + 0.65 × trend_yesterday)
// where raw = max(load, soreness)

When a cell shows —

Load needs that pattern trained on at least three dates spanning 28 days in the prior 180. Soreness needs today's rating. Trend needs both, plus three consecutive check-ins; a gap of more than seven days restarts it. These are provisional display rules, not measured recovery times.

How pattern labels are derived

Pattern labels are not stored as a separate string in exercise_mappings. The app derives them from the pct columns using the movement split stored in the mapping table:

ConditionLabel

Tie-break order

If two or more pct columns are tied for the maximum value, the app uses insertion order to break the tie: Knee → Hip → Push → Pull.

06b

Canonical exercise names

The canonical naming tool lets you assign a preferred display name to any exercise, overriding whatever name Hevy uses. This is useful when Hevy's default exercise names don't match your naming convention, or when you've renamed a custom exercise in Hevy but want Hevy Fatigue to reflect the new name immediately.

How it works

Hevy Fatigue tracks exercises by their internal Hevy ID, not by title. When you set a canonical name for an exercise, Hevy Fatigue stores the alias and uses it everywhere — in Movement Trend, exercise mappings, and stress calculations — regardless of what name Hevy sends during sync.

When to use it

  • A default Hevy exercise has a name you want to standardize (e.g. "Incline Bench Press (Dumbbells)" → "Bench Press, Dumbbell, Incline")
  • You renamed a custom exercise in Hevy but Hevy Fatigue still shows the old name
  • You want all historical data for an exercise to reflect a new name without waiting for a future workout to sync

How to set a canonical name

  1. Open Menu → Patterns
  2. Find the exercise and click Edit
  3. Enter your preferred name in the Canonical Name field
  4. Save — Hevy Fatigue immediately updates all historical workout data for that exercise to the canonical name

When Hevy sends a different name for an exercise you already have, it appears under Exercise Names — Needs Review at the top of Menu → Exercises (the Exercises entry shows a badge). From there you can set a canonical name to lock it, dismiss the change, or use Add Override to name any exercise.

What happens on sync

Every time Hevy Fatigue syncs with Hevy, incoming exercise titles are checked against the canonical table by exercise ID. If a canonical name exists, it overwrites whatever title Hevy sent — so the canonical name always wins.

Clearing a canonical name

Leave the Canonical Name field blank and save. Hevy Fatigue removes the alias and reverts to using whatever title Hevy sends on the next sync. Your pattern mapping is preserved.

Limitations
  • Canonical names only work for exercises that have been synced at least once — Hevy Fatigue needs the Hevy exercise ID to create the alias.
  • Exercises with a null exercise ID from the Hevy API cannot be canonically named. These are rare but will be flagged in the exercise list.

06d

Vol-Fatigue correlation

The Volume · Fatigue card on the dashboard shows daily training volume next to how fatigued you reported feeling, over a selected block window. It is a reading tool - it shows the relationship between work done and how the body responded, without computing thresholds or prescribing adjustments.

Signal toggle

Three signals are available via toggle:

  • Tonnage (default) - sum of weight_lbs × reps across all sets for the day. Zero on rest days.
  • RPE Load - the day's session stress (central + peripheral, see §04), which depends on RPE and reps rather than weight. Zero on rest days.
  • Sets - total sets logged for the day. Zero on rest days.

Chart format

The selected volume signal renders as vertical bars. Rest days appear as zero-height bars - they are honest gaps in training, not interpolated values. A dashed Fatigue line on the right Y-axis (0–20, higher = more fatigued) shows the 7-day average of your subjective check-in score. It is left blank until a 7-day window holds at least three check-ins.

Block window

The date range is user-selectable: 4 weeks, 8 weeks, 12 weeks, or a custom date range. Changing the window re-fetches from GET /api/volfatigue/summary.

Interpretation note

Fatigue rising after a high-volume period is expected - the 7-day average makes the line lag peak training by a few days. That lag is the signal you are looking for: fatigue that consistently climbs after high-volume periods shows your personal stress ceiling over time.

07

EWMA — ATL, CTL, and the time constants

The core of the load model is an Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA). Two EWMAs with different decay rates are computed in parallel:

MetricFull nameTime constant (τ)What it captures
ATLAcute Training Load7 daysRecent fatigue / short-term stress
CTLChronic Training Load42 daysFitness / long-term training capacity

EWMA recurrence formula

// Decay factor from time constant τ (days)
λ = 2 / (τ + 1)

// EWMA update rule (run daily)
EWMA_today = load_today × λ + EWMA_yesterday × (1 - λ)

// For ATL: λ = 2/(7+1) = 0.25
// For CTL: λ = 2/(42+1) ≈ 0.0465

On rest days, load_today = 0, so both ATL and CTL decay toward zero over time. ATL decays quickly (7-day window), CTL decays slowly (42-day window).

Origin of these constants

The 7-day and 42-day time constants derive from Banister's Impulse-Response model [5], adapted into training software by Coggan and Allen [6] for cycling power data. They are widely used heuristics — not derived from first principles — and their validity has been debated in the literature [7]. This system uses them as a reasonable starting point, not as precise biological claims.

Stabilization period

Because CTL has a 42-day window, the model requires approximately 6–8 weeks of consistent check-in history before CTL values are meaningful. Early CTL readings are systematically underestimated (the EWMA hasn't "filled up" yet). Values should be interpreted with caution during this ramp-up period.

Important

CTL is still building during the initial deployment period. Numbers will stabilize meaningfully after 6–8 weeks of consistent data. Early TSB readings should be treated as directional, not precise.

08

TSB & training state

Training Stress Balance (TSB)

TSB is the difference between fitness (CTL) and fatigue (ATL). It represents the net "readiness" signal from the load model alone:

TSB = CTL - ATL
TSB rangeTraining state
≥ +8Underloaded
+3 to < +8Slightly Fresh
-5 to < +3Balanced
-10 to < -5Slightly Fatigued
< -10Fatigued

Training state labels

Training state labels are resolved from TSB thresholds in the API settings. They describe load balance relative to recent fitness, not a directive:

Underloaded
Fitness is significantly outpacing recent training stress.
Slightly Fresh
Fitness outpacing fatigue; recovery is ahead of training.
Balanced
Fitness and fatigue are well matched; normal training state.
Slightly Fatigued
Fatigue is outpacing fitness; accumulated stress is present.
Fatigued
Fatigue is significantly elevated relative to current fitness.
Display note

TSB is computed internally and drives the pattern stress signals and readiness model, but is not directly displayed on the dashboard. Athletes who want to inspect raw ATL/CTL/TSB values can access them via the Diagnostics page. The dashboard's primary daily signal is the fatigue score instead - an 80/20 blend of your subjective check-in and recent tonnage (see §08b).

08b

Fatigue score display

The Readiness card shows the fatigue score as X / 20, where higher means more fatigued. The big number is the smoothed fatigue trend; underneath it, the Subjective, Objective and Combined scores show today's raw parts. The big number shows — until monitoring is established.

Objective load score

The objective component compares the last seven completed days of tonnage against your 180-day weekly average, on the same 0–20 scale. Today's training is left out, so this morning's check-in is read against the work that came before it.

// Recent: tonnage (weight_lbs × reps) over the 7 completed days before today
// Baseline: tonnage over the 180 days before today ÷ 26 weeks
ratio = recent_tonnage / baseline_weekly_avg

// Scale to 0–20: ratio of 1.0 (normal week) → 10.0
objective_score = min(ratio × 10, 20)
RatioObjective scoreMeaning
0.00.0No training in the last seven days
1.010.0Training exactly at your usual weekly volume
2.0 or more20.0 (capped)Double your usual weekly volume
Scientific basis — ACWR

The Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio is the most widely cited load monitoring metric in applied sports science (Gabbett, 2016 [10]). The standard implementation uses a 1:4 week window. This app uses a 1:26 week window (7-day acute vs. 6-month chronic baseline) — a more conservative choice that smooths across full training blocks rather than individual phases. The 6-month window is intentional: it gives a stable long-term reference for the subjective-vs-objective comparison rather than reacting to recent block structure.

Why tonnage, not session stress scores

The objective score deliberately uses raw tonnage (weight × reps) rather than the session stress scores that feed ATL/CTL/TSB. This is by design: the long-term purpose of this signal is to build the longitudinal volume record needed for MRV/MEV analysis. Session stress scores are appropriate for acute fatigue modeling; tonnage is appropriate for volume tracking and eventual volume threshold work. The two systems serve different purposes and operate on different foundations intentionally.

Combined score

The subjective and objective scores are blended with an 80/20 weighting that keeps the subjective signal firmly in control:

combined_score = (0.80 × subjective_score) + (0.20 × objective_score)
Scientific basis — subjective-first weighting

The 80/20 split is a practitioner-selected design decision, not statistically derived from individual data. It reflects the documented principle that subjective wellness measures are often more predictive of performance decrements than objective load metrics alone — a finding supported directionally in the athlete monitoring literature (McLean et al., 2010 [11]). Weights are assumed, not validated; full psychometric validation would require structured longitudinal criterion data that is not feasible in a personal tool. Face validity and longitudinal self-comparison are the appropriate standards here.

Fatigue trend (smoothing)

The headline number smooths the combined score so one good morning doesn't swing the recommendation. Worse days count in full straight away; better days pull the trend down gradually.

// Rises immediately, recovers 35% of the way each day
trend_today = max(combined, 0.35 × combined + 0.65 × trend_yesterday)

// A gap of more than 7 days restarts the trend from that day's score

Recommendation bands

Fatigue trendRecommendation
6 or belowLarge Increase — low fatigue, push hard and increase training load
above 6 to 8Increase — below baseline, good time to add volume or intensity
above 8 to 13Continue — training load well managed
above 13 to 14Decrease — fatigue elevated, reduce volume or intensity today
above 14Large Decrease — high fatigue, rest or very light activity only

A worse band takes effect immediately. Moving to a better band needs two consecutive check-ins that sit at least 0.5 below the band edge, and it moves one band at a time. Until monitoring is established — three consecutive complete check-ins, and at least three training dates spanning 28 days in the last 180 — the card shows Continue Training as Normal with the reason.

08c

Fatigue Trend chart

The dashboard's Fatigue Trend chart covers the past 7 days, with short weekday labels and a 0–20 y-axis (higher = more fatigued).

  • Solid line — the smoothed fatigue trend (the headline number from §08b).
  • Dashed line — the raw combined score for each day.
  • Shading — your usual range: the 10th–90th percentile of your own trend over the prior 84 days.

Days without a check-in are gaps; the line breaks instead of joining across missing data. Hovering a day shows its combined, objective and subjective scores and where it sat against your usual range.

Usual range

The shading appears once there are at least 14 valid days spanning 28 days. Until then the chart says the personal range is still collecting. Each day's range uses only earlier days. It describes what is normal for you; it is not a target or an optimal recovery zone, and it never changes the score or the recommendation.

Data source

The chart is populated from GET /api/readiness/combined-history, the same model that powers the Readiness card.

Dashboard layout

The Dashboard is the landing page. Cards are arranged in a grid on wide screens and stack in a single column on a phone, in this order:

  • Weekly review notice — appears only when a planned week (microcycle) is finished, with a link to review it in AI Coach.
  • Coaching call banner — appears only when a joint rating has been above your usual for 3 of your last 7 check-ins, with a link to the call; after a "carry on" decision it shrinks to one quiet line (see §09).

It is grouped into Overview (Dashboard, AI Coach), Training (Log Workout, Plans, Recent, Progress, Stress, RPE Charts), Monitoring (Exercises, Workouts, Check-in History, Patterns) and Tools (Docs, Settings). Joint health advisory

Joint health is collected as two separate fields (joint_upper and joint_lower) on a 0-4 scale. It is processed separately from the fatigue formula.

On the dashboard, joint health appears as a standalone advisory indicator. It is interpreted as a tissue loading signal:

Joint score (0-4)Advisory levelDashboard behavior
0-2noneNo joint warning row is shown.
3advisoryJoint advisory row is shown for the affected region.
4warningHigher-severity advisory row is shown for the affected region.

The advisory is informational only. The system makes no assumptions about which joints are affected or which exercises should be modified — that judgment belongs to the athlete.

Your usual level and coaching calls

Each region also has a usual rating: the rating you gave most often over the last 28 days, from at least 7 check-ins. A tie goes to the lower rating. Check-ins from a flare-up never count toward usual, so a lasting rise can't quietly become your normal. Usual moves up only when you choose This is my new normal in a coaching call; it moves down on its own.

Check-ins above usualWhat happens
1–2Recorded only. The daily coach stays quiet.
3 of your last 7 (within 14 days)A Coaching call needed banner appears on the Dashboard. Loads are held on that region's lifts (lower: knee and hip; upper: push and pull): the Log page shows a hold note and suggests no increases. Planned weights are not changed.
5 of your last 10 (within 21 days)The call focuses on reviewing the movement.
Any rating of 3 or 4The advisory above, straight away, as before.

The call opens in AI Coach (or inside your weekly coaching call when both are due). The coach helps you work out the likely cause; you record the decision on the card under the call:

  • This is my new normal — usual moves up to the raised level and the count restarts.
  • Carry on until… — defaults to one week. Usual stays put and the hold lifts; the call comes back on that date, or earlier if a rating reaches 3.
  • Make a change — note what you changed (variation, range of motion, swap or lighter load). The count restarts from that day.
  • Not now — the banner and the hold stay.

The notice clears on its own once the count drops below these levels.

Joint evidence (Patterns page)

The Patterns page compares your joint rating at the first check-in after days that included a lift with all your other check-ins over the last 12 weeks, for each lift and, when two or more lifts share a pattern, for the pattern overall. Lifts from the app's own logger and from Hevy both count. A line needs at least 3 check-ins after the lift; below 6 it's marked early read. It shows what tends to go together, not proof of cause. The fatigue trial keeps its own joint rule.

10b

AI Coach

Menu → AI Coach is a chat for training questions. It is a backend-proxied OpenRouter client: your API key is stored encrypted server-side and is never exposed to the browser.

  • Review my training starts a review of the last six months of training. Saved reviews can be reopened from the drop-down next to it.
  • Available equipment lists what you can train with, plus notes on limits. The coach only proposes movements you have checked; anything left unset makes it ask rather than assume.
  • show context previews the training context that is sent to the model with your message.
  • Any program change the coach suggests is a proposal only; nothing changes until you accept it.

The coach also appears on the Dashboard: a short daily recommendation on the Readiness card after you check in, and a weekly review notice when a planned week is finished.

The model picker offers preset OpenRouter models plus a custom model input; your choice is remembered in this browser. Replies are rendered with marked.js, so markdown formatting is preserved.

Requirement

The AI Coach requires an OpenRouter API key configured in Settings before it can send messages.

Known limitations

ATL/CTL/TSB and strength sport: The EWMA model and its 7/42-day time constants were developed for continuous endurance load patterns (cycling power data). Strength training is more intermittent and session stress is more heterogeneous — a 1RM attempt and a hypertrophy pump session may have similar tonnage but very different systemic demands. ATL/CTL/TSB are retained for trend visibility and pattern stress signals but are acknowledged as imperfect tools for strength sport. The subjective check-in score is the primary readiness driver; ATL/CTL/TSB are contextual modifiers.

EWMA stabilization: Both ATL and CTL require historical data to stabilize. Expect 2–3 weeks before ATL is reliable and 6–8 weeks before CTL is meaningful. Early TSB values are directional only.

sRPE accuracy: Session RPE is a subjective self-report and is known to be influenced by mood, expectations, and recall timing. Foster et al. recommend collecting sRPE 30 minutes post-session to reduce bias [4].

Conditioning scaling: The factor k used to normalize conditioning load to strength load units is a calibration estimate. It should be revisited after several weeks of mixed-modality data.

Individual variation: All time constants (7-day ATL, 42-day CTL) are population-level heuristics. Individual adaptation rates vary — some athletes recover faster, some slower. These defaults are a starting point.

Exercise classification: Automatic movement pattern classification relies on exercise name matching. Novel exercises, variant naming, or exercises not in the classification dictionary may be misclassified or unclassified.

No injury prediction: This system does not predict injury risk. Joint health advisory scores are self-reported and are informational context, not diagnostic output.
